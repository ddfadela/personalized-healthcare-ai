\section{Enhancing Heart Disease Prediction with Reinforcement Learning and Data Augmentation}
\textbf{Article Reference:} \cite{gayathriEnhancingHeartDisease2024}

\subsection*{Overview}
This study aims to improve the prediction accuracy of heart disease by integrating reinforcement learning (RL) and data augmentation techniques. The approach addresses the complexities of cardiac data, which often hampers traditional machine learning models, by leveraging advanced methods to enhance predictive performance and early diagnosis.

\subsection*{Dataset}
The primary dataset employed is similar to the Cleveland Heart Disease dataset, sourced from the UCI Machine Learning Repository. It contains features such as age, gender, blood pressure, cholesterol levels, ECG results, and other patient health indicators. The dataset includes a target variable indicating the presence or absence of heart disease, facilitating classification tasks. Additional datasets might come from healthcare agencies and research repositories.

\subsection*{Data Processing}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Feature Selection:} Techniques such as feature importance scores and recursive feature elimination were used to identify the most impactful variables for heart disease prediction.
    \item \textbf{Data Augmentation:} Applied transformations like feature scaling, rotation, noise addition, and synthetic data generation to expand and diversify the training data. This helps models handle variability and reduce overfitting.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Approach}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reinforcement Learning (RL):} Utilizing RL algorithms to optimize decision-making processes dynamically, allowing models to adapt to evolving patient data and improve prediction accuracy over time.
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{How It Functions in the Study:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Initialization:}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item The RL agent starts with an initial policy, possibly based on prior knowledge or random actions.
            \item The dataset is preprocessed, and the model's initial parameters are set.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Interaction Loop:}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item For each episode, the agent:
            \begin{itemize}
                \item Observes the current state (e.g., patient features).
                \item Selects an action according to its policy (e.g., choosing a specific augmentation or parameter setting).
                \item Executes the action, which may involve training the model further, updating parameters, or selecting data augmentation techniques.
                \item Moves to the next state, reflecting the outcome of its action, such as improved data representation or better predictive performance.
                \item Receives a reward based on the effectiveness of its action, such as increased accuracy or better generalization.
            \end{itemize}
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Learning:}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item The agent updates its policy based on the feedback (rewards), aiming to improve decision-making over future episodes.
            \item Techniques like Q-learning or policy gradients are often used to optimize this process.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Outcome:}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Over many iterations, the RL model learns which actions lead to higher rewards and adapts its strategy to improve heart disease prediction accuracy continually.
        \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\noindent In summary:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{States} represent patient data or model status.
    \item \textbf{Actions} correspond to decisions like data augmentation choices or model updates.
    \item \textbf{Rewards} are signals (e.g., accuracy improvements) guiding the learning process.
    \item The RL agent learns the best policy to update the model continuously, maximizing prediction performance.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Results}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Achieved an accuracy rate of approximately 94\%, surpassing traditional models
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Challenges}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Computational Complexity:} The combined methods demand significant processing power and longer training times.
    \item \textbf{Data Quality and Accessibility:} The efficacy of data augmentation depends heavily on the quality of the original dataset; biases or missing data can impact outcomes.
    \item \textbf{Model Generalizability:} Design choices and assumptions within the RL framework may limit applicability across diverse patient populations or clinical settings.
    \item \textbf{Scalability:} Handling large-scale, real-world datasets remains challenging due to resource requirements.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Future Directions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fine-tuning Techniques:} Further optimizing model parameters and augmentation strategies.
    \item \textbf{Privacy and Security:} Incorporating mechanisms to ensure patient data privacy.
    \item \textbf{Clinical Validation:} Conducting extensive real-world clinical trials to validate model usefulness and safety.
    \item \textbf{Broader Application:} Extending the methodology to other medical diagnostic areas beyond heart disease.
    \item \textbf{Reducing Computational Costs:} Developing more efficient algorithms to make the approach more scalable and practical in healthcare settings.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Critique}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The integration of RL and data augmentation is promising, but computational demands and reliance on data quality could hinder deployment in resource-limited settings.
    \item The paper lacks details on the exact RL algorithm used, which is essential for reproducibility.
    \item There is limited discussion on how interpretability is addressed, which is crucial for clinical use.
\end{itemize}

\section{A Reinforcement Learning–Based Method for Management of Type 1 Diabetes: Exploratory Study}
\textbf{Article Reference:} \cite{javadReinforcementLearningBased2019}

\subsection*{Overview}
The researchers developed a reinforcement learning (RL) framework, specifically a Q-learning algorithm, to personalize insulin dosing for patients with Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM). The aim was to improve blood glucose management by recommending insulin doses tailored to individual patient characteristics.

\subsection*{Dataset}
The dataset consisted of clinical records from 87 T1DM patients treated at Mass General Hospital (MGH) between 2003 and 2013.

The data included patient information such as HbA1c levels, BMI, physical activity, and alcohol usage.

The authors conducted a correlation analysis to identify key variables influencing blood glucose, concluding that HbA1c, BMI, activity level, and alcohol usage were the most relevant.

Based on these factors, they defined the patient’s state by discretizing these variables into levels:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item HbA1c levels (e.g., normal, elevated, high)
        \item BMI categories
        \item Activity levels (e.g., low, high)
        \item Alcohol usage levels (e.g., none, moderate, high)
    \end{itemize}

\subsection*{Data Processing}
States were formed as combinations of these discretized features.

Insulin doses (actions) were defined within specific ranges (e.g., Lantus dose intervals).

The data was used to train and validate the RL model.

\subsection*{Approach}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The Q-learning algorithm was employed, which is a model-free RL method.
    \item The environment is represented by the patient’s health state, and the agent makes decisions on insulin dosage.
    \item The states are defined by the combination of HbA1c, BMI, activity level, and alcohol usage.
    \item The actions are discretized insulin dose levels (specific dose intervals).
    \item The reward function is designed based on how well the insulin dose achieved the target HbA1c level.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Approach}
\begin{itemize}
    \item At each time step (e.g., clinical visit), the agent observes the state and selects an action (insulin dose) either by exploration (random choice with probability $\epsilon$) or exploitation (based on learned Q-values).
    \item After administering the dose, the patient's response (e.g., change in HbA1c) results in a reward, guiding the learning process.
    \item The Q-values are updated iteratively based on the reward and the estimated value of subsequent states.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Results}
\begin{itemize}
    \item RL model tested on 60 unseen cases
    \item Recommended insulin dose interval included the physician-prescribed dose in 88\% of cases
    \item Results suggest the RL approach can effectively offer personalized treatment recommendations aligning with clinical decisions
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Challenges}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Limited dataset size:} Only 87 patients, which may limit generalizability
    \item \textbf{Discretization of variables:} Fineness of categories might affect the model's precision
    \item \textbf{Data quality and missing variables:} Not all potentially influential factors (like diet or stress) were included
    \item \textbf{Algorithm complexity:} RL models require careful tuning; real-world implementation must address issues like exploration vs. exploitation and patient safety
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Future Directions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Extend the model to include other types of insulin and medications
    \item Incorporate finer categories or continuous variables for more precise recommendations
    \item Validate with larger and more diverse datasets
    \item Explore application to other populations, such as patients with Type 2 Diabetes
    \item Implement real-time decision support in clinical settings
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Critique}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Limited patient sample size may not capture all variability in diabetes management
    \item Discretization may lead to loss of information that could be valuable for precise dosing
    \item No explicit mention of model validation techniques beyond testing on 60 cases
    \item Reward function description is brief; more detail would clarify alignment with clinical goals
\end{itemize}

The paper addresses the challenges of using deep learning models for disease prediction with EHRs, including lack of precision, ethical concerns, limitations of small datasets, complexity of data processing, and incompleteness of patient data. The model integrates reinforcement learning with neural networks, utilizing the mapping capabilities of the Q-network. The proposed model is evaluated on the Heart Disease Dataset from the UCI Data Repository, demonstrating high accuracy (98\%) compared to other models.

\subsection*{Dataset}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The Heart Disease Dataset from the UCI Data Repository via Kaggle.
    \item Includes multivariate numerical data with 14 attributes (categorical, integer, and real data).
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Data Processing}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The dataset was split into training (80\%) and test sets (20\%) using stratified splitting.
    \item Robust scaling was applied for feature scaling.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Approach}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The study uses a Deep Q-Network (DQN) model, which combines reinforcement learning with neural networks.
    \item This approach aims to address the limitations of traditional Q-learning and improve the accuracy and stability of disease predictions.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Implementation of Reinforcement Learning}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The model uses a "disease prediction game" environment.
    \item State: The state is represented by the samples from the EHR data.
    \item Action: Actions involve increasing, decreasing, or holding each feature.
    \item Reward: Positive rewards are given for accurate predictions, and negative rewards for inaccurate ones.
    \item Environment: The environment consists of sets of states (samples) and actions (feature adjustments).
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Results}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The paper compares the Deep Q-Network (DQN) model with Logistic Regression, Decision Tree Classifier, Random Forest Classifier, and Gradient-Boosting Classifier.
\end{itemize}

Key Results:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The proposed EHR-DQN model achieved high accuracy (0.9841) and a low mean squared error (MSE) of 0.0001.
    \item The Decision Tree and Gradient-Boosting Classifiers also performed well, with perfect precision, recall, and F1 scores.
    \item The Random Forest model showed a balance of high accuracy (0.9783) and a minimal MSE of 0.
    \item Logistic Regression had the lowest performance, with an accuracy of 0.8424 and a higher MSE of 0.1576.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Challenges}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Data-related issues: digitization, consolidation, and availability of health records.
    \item Privacy and legal concerns with patient data handling.
    \item Patient-related difficulties: decision-making errors, treatment errors, and data inconsistencies.
    \item Technical challenges: data integration across systems, ensuring patient security.
    \item Interpretability: "black box" nature of complex AI models limits transparency.
    \item Resource limitations: computational resources and expertise needed for implementation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Future Directions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Expand the dataset size to better train deep reinforcement learning models.
    \item Integrate additional contextual features to improve prediction accuracy.
    \item Develop more transparent models to address interpretability concerns.
    \item Create more standardized methods for validating healthcare AI systems.
    \item Improve integration with clinical workflows for practical implementation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Critique}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The paper emphasizes the high accuracy of the proposed model but also points out that the dataset used has only thousands of samples. The DQN algorithm typically requires a large number of training samples (millions) to achieve optimal performance.
    \item The paper acknowledges the "black box" nature of many AI algorithms and the resulting lack of interpretability, which can hinder trust and adoption in healthcare.
    \item Needs hyper-parameter tuning
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}

\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
    \item They utilized the publicly available HAM10000 dataset, a comprehensive collection of dermatoscopic images of skin lesions.
    \item This dataset contains over 10,000 images across seven diagnostic categories of skin lesions.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Data Processing}
\begin{itemize}
    \item For patient-centered scenarios, input vectors were normalized by dividing position-wise by the average across all lesion vectors of the same patient.

\subsection*{Results}

    \item The RL method and threshold method both improved management decisions compared to the naïve SL model.
    \item Both approaches optimized operating points on decision curves, enhancing diagnostic accuracy.
    \item The RL model demonstrated superior performance in balancing sensitivity and specificity.
    \item The study showed potential for reducing unnecessary excisions while maintaining high detection rates.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Challenges}
\begin{itemize}
    \item RL models require more complex retraining compared to simpler threshold approaches.
    \item Integration into clinical workflows presents practical implementation barriers.

    \item Lack of longitudinal data to validate long-term decision outcomes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Future Directions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Development of reward tables incorporating both physician and patient preferences.
    \item Enhancement of shared decision-making tools combining AI recommendations with clinical expertise.
    \item Integration of additional clinical parameters beyond image data.
    \item Validation in prospective clinical trials to assess real-world impact.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Critique}
\begin{itemize}
    \item It focuses primarily on physician preferences, potentially neglecting patient-centered care principles.
    \item Limited dataset diversity may restrict generalizability across different patient populations.

\section{Reinforcement Learning Using Deep Q Networks and Q-Learning Accurately Localizes Brain Tumors on MRI with Very Small Training Sets}
\textbf{Article Reference:} \cite{stemberReinforcementLearningUsing2022}

\subsection*{Overview}
This study applies reinforcement learning (RL), specifically Deep Q Networks (DQN), to accurately localize brain tumors in MRI scans. It addresses the limitations of traditional supervised learning methods, particularly the dependence on large annotated datasets. The authors demonstrate that RL can achieve high performance even with minimal training data.

\subsection*{Dataset}
The dataset used comprises 2D slices from the 2014 BraTS (Brain Tumor Segmentation) challenge dataset. These are T1-weighted contrast-enhanced MRI images. The training set included 30 images, with another 30 images used for testing.

\subsection*{Data Processing}
\begin{itemize}
    \item 2D slices were extracted and the image space was divided into a grid.
    \item Each agent operates within a 60 × 60 pixel block.
    \item No data augmentation was applied to keep training consistent with the original dataset.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Implementation of Reinforcement Learning}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Environment:} Modeled as a gridworld over MRI images.
    \item \textbf{States:} Represented by the agent's position in the image grid.
    \item \textbf{Actions:} The agent can move down, move right, or stay in place.
    \item \textbf{Rewards:} Positive rewards for entering tumor regions, penalties for remaining idle outside the tumor area.
    \item The DQN used experience replay and a target network to stabilize learning.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Results}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The DQN achieved an average localization accuracy of 70\% over the last 20 episodes.
    \item This significantly outperformed a supervised deep learning approach, which achieved only 11\% accuracy.
    \item Results demonstrate RL’s superiority with small datasets in this context.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Challenges}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The reliance on small datasets may limit generalizability.
    \item High computational cost of RL may pose integration challenges in real-time clinical settings.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Future Directions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Extend the RL model to handle full 3D MRI volumes.
    \item Compare performance with other RL strategies such as policy-gradient methods.
    \item Optimize training and inference to improve clinical applicability.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Critique}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The model's performance with small data is promising but needs validation on larger, more diverse datasets.
    \item No integration of clinical context or physician-in-the-loop evaluation.
    \item Despite excellent performance metrics, interpretability and ease of integration in hospitals remain unresolved.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Future Directions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Development of measurement technologies informed by simulation findings.

        \item \textbf{Neural Controlled Differential Equations (CDE)}: Continuous-time neural architecture for irregular time series modeling
        \item \textbf{Decoupled Dynamics Module (DDM)}: Separated modeling of state dynamics and observation processes
        \item \textbf{Deep Signature Transforms (DST)}: Path signature-based representation for sequential data characterization
        \item \textbf{ODE-RNN (ODE)}: Ordinary differential equation enhanced recurrent networks for continuous dynamics
        \item \textbf{Simple Autoencoder (AE)*}*: Non-recurrent baseline autoencoder for comparison
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mathematical Formulation}: Learning encoding function that maps patient history H\textsubscript{t,t-1} to state representation S\textsubscript{t}, and decoding function predicting subsequent observation O\textsubscript{t+1} given state S\textsubscript{t} and action A\textsubscript{t}.
    \item \textbf{Batch Constrained Q-Learning (dBCQ)}: Off-policy RL algorithm implementation suitable for offline learning with observational healthcare data, addressing extrapolation errors through Q-function truncation for out-of-distribution actions.
    \item \textbf{Representation Enhancement Techniques}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Demographic Context Integration}: Incorporation of static demographic features as encoder inputs to improve representation quality
        \item \textbf{Correlation Regularization}: Loss function regularization using correlation with clinical acuity scores (SOFA, SAPS II, OASIS) to encourage clinically meaningful representations
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Policy Evaluation}: Weighted Importance Sampling (WIS) methodology for policy performance assessment on held-out test data, providing robust offline evaluation metrics.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Results}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Representation Dimensionality Effects}: Prediction accuracy for subsequent observations saturates beyond 64-dimensional representations, with higher dimensions sometimes degrading test performance, indicating that increased model capacity does not guarantee improved informativeness.
    \item \textbf{Demographic Context Benefits}: Consistent improvement in subsequent observation prediction performance when demographic features are included as encoder inputs, demonstrating the value of static patient characteristics for dynamic state modeling.
    \item \textbf{Regularization Impact}: Correlation regularization with clinical acuity scores generally degrades performance except for DDM and DST architectures. Unregularized representations often fail to encode acuity-correlated information, but demographic context inclusion improves such correlations.
    \item \textbf{Architecture Performance Rankings}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Top Performer}: Neural CDE (CDE) encoded representations consistently produce superior downstream policies
        \item \textbf{Strong Performers}: Simpler recurrent architectures (AIS, RNN) outperform non-recurrent autoencoders
        \item \textbf{Context Dependency}: Best-performing policies across most architectures utilize demographic context during representation learning
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical Interpretability}: Learned representations, particularly with demographic context, provide enhanced separability between patients with different outcomes (recovery vs. mortality) compared to raw observational data, suggesting clinically meaningful feature extraction.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Challenges}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Representation Capacity Paradox}: Higher-dimensional representations, despite increased theoretical capacity, can reduce prediction accuracy and policy performance, challenging conventional assumptions about model complexity and effectiveness.
    \item \textbf{Clinical Regularization Limitations}: Regularization approaches designed to incorporate clinical knowledge through acuity score correlation do not consistently improve representation quality or downstream policy performance, questioning direct clinical knowledge integration strategies.
    \item \textbf{Information Content Opacity}: Limited understanding of what information is actually encoded in learned representations and how this relates to clinical decision-making processes, creating interpretability challenges for clinical deployment.
    \item \textbf{Demographic Bias Concerns}: Incorporation of demographic information, while improving performance, raises potential fairness and bias issues that require careful consideration in clinical applications.
    \item \textbf{Generalizability Questions}: Uncertainty regarding whether learned representations capture generalizable patient health trends (improvement/deterioration) beyond immediate observation prediction capabilities.
    \item \textbf{Sequential Data Complexity}: Healthcare data's inherently partial, irregular, and noisy nature poses persistent challenges for representation learning architectures designed for more structured sequential data.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Future Directions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Multi-Task Learning Integration}: Development of joint training approaches that simultaneously optimize representation learning and multiple clinical prediction tasks to improve overall system performance and generalizability.
    \item \textbf{Missingness-Aware Methods}: Investigation of architectures that explicitly model feature missingness patterns and incorporate missingness indicators as informative signals rather than obstacles to overcome.
    \item \textbf{Bias Quantification Studies}: Systematic analysis of demographic information encoding effects on representation space bias and quantification of associated performance trade-offs across different patient populations.
    \item \textbf{End-to-End Policy Development}: Creation of unified frameworks combining auxiliary prediction tasks with RL objectives, explicitly accounting for state representation quality in policy optimization processes.
    \item \textbf{Clinical Interpretability Enhancement}: Development of methods for understanding and visualizing what clinical relationships and patterns are preserved or discovered in learned state representations.
    \item \textbf{Temporal Trend Embedding}: Investigation of whether representations can effectively capture longitudinal patient health trajectories and disease progression patterns beyond immediate next-step prediction.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Critique}
\begin{itemize}
    \item This study provides a comprehensive and methodologically rigorous empirical comparison of representation learning architectures for healthcare RL, addressing a critical gap in understanding optimal state encoding strategies for clinical decision-making.
    \item The systematic evaluation across multiple architectures with consistent experimental design and evaluation metrics provides valuable insights for the healthcare AI community and establishes important benchmarks for future research.

    \item Primary limitations include the study's restriction to sepsis treatment scenarios, potentially limiting generalizability to other clinical domains, and the reliance on offline evaluation methods that may not fully capture real-world deployment challenges.

\section{Supervised-actor-critic reinforcement learning for intelligent mechanical ventilation and sedative dosing in intensive care units}
\textbf{Article Reference:} \cite{yuSupervisedactorcriticReinforcementLearning2020}

\subsection*{Overview and Objective}
This paper proposes a Supervised-Actor-Critic (SAC) reinforcement learning algorithm for addressing decision-making problems in Intensive Care Units (ICUs), specifically for mechanical ventilation and sedative dosing. The key innovation is combining long-term goal-oriented RL with short-term supervised learning objectives to ensure patient safety during treatment while optimizing clinical outcomes. The approach aims to cure patients in the long term while keeping treatment strategies aligned with clinical expert guidance.

\subsection*{Problem Statement}
Traditional RL algorithms focus solely on maximizing long-term rewards, which can lead to exploration strategies that may pose fatal risks to patients. Clinical treatment requires both:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Long-term goal:} Curing the patient
    \item \textbf{Short-term goal:} Maintaining patient stability through proper daily dosage control
\end{itemize}
The challenge is that traditional RL may achieve high cumulative rewards but deviate significantly from safe clinical treatment strategies.

\subsection*{Dataset Used}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Source:} MIMIC-III database (Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care)
    \item \textbf{Patients:} 8,860 adult patient admissions extracted
    \item \textbf{Inclusion Criteria:} Patients kept under ventilation for at least 24 hours and successfully discharged from ICU
    \item \textbf{Patient Demographics:} Median age 65.8 years, 55.9\% male, 11.5\% hospitalized
    \item \textbf{Temporal Resolution:} 10-minute intervals from admission to discharge
    \item \textbf{Preprocessing:} Support Vector Machines (SVM) used to fit physiological measurements at different times
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{MDP Formulation}
The decision-making problem is modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP):

\subsubsection*{State (13-dimensional features)}
Respiration rate, heart rate, arterial pH, PEEP set, SpO2, FiO2, arterial oxygen partial pressure, plateau pressure, average airway pressure, mean non-invasive blood pressure, body weight, and age.

\subsubsection*{Action}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ventilation:} Binary decision (wean off or continue ventilation)
    \item \textbf{Sedative:} Four discrete propofol dose levels
    \item \textbf{Total:} Eight action combinations
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Reward Function}
\begin{equation}
r_{t+1} = r^{vitals}_{t+1} + r^{vent\ off}_{t+1} + r^{vent\ on}_{t+1}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $r^{vitals}_{t+1}$: Evaluates physiological stability
    \item $r^{vent\ off}_{t+1}$: Performance of stopping ventilation
    \item $r^{vent\ on}_{t+1}$: Cost per hour on ventilator
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Supervised-Actor-Critic (SAC) Algorithm}

\subsubsection*{Framework Components}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Actor Network:} Generates treatment actions
    \item \textbf{Critic Network:} Evaluates action-state values using TD error
    \item \textbf{Supervisor Network:} Provides expert guidance to reduce deviation from clinician strategies
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Actor Network Update}
Combined objective function:
\begin{equation}
J(\theta) = (1 - \epsilon)J_{RL}(\theta) + \epsilon(-J_{SL}(\theta))
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $J_{RL}(\theta)$: RL optimization goal (maximize expected reward)
    \item $J_{SL}(\theta)$: Supervised learning goal (minimize deviation from expert actions)
    \item $\epsilon$: Weighting parameter balancing RL and supervised learning
\end{itemize}

Parameter update using stochastic gradient descent:
\begin{equation}
\theta = \theta + \alpha\left((1-\epsilon)\frac{\partial J_{RL}(\theta)}{\partial\theta} + \epsilon\left(-\frac{\partial J_{SL}(\theta)}{\partial\theta}\right)\right)
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Critic Network Update}
\begin{equation}
J(w) = E_{r_t,s_t}\left[(Q_w(s_t,a_t) - y_t)^2\right]
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
y_t = r(s_t,a_t) + \gamma Q^{tar}_w(s_{t+1}, \mu_\theta(s_{t+1}))
\end{equation}

\subsection*{Network Architecture}
All networks (Actor, Critic, Supervisor) use three-layer neural networks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Actor:} 20 hidden neurons (ReLU), 2 output neurons (Softmax)
    \item \textbf{Critic:} 20 hidden neurons (ReLU), 1 output neuron (no activation)
    \item \textbf{Supervisor:} 9 hidden neurons, 2 output neurons (Softmax)
    \item \textbf{Encoding:} One-hot encoding for discrete actions
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Experimental Results}

\subsubsection*{Ventilation Experiment}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Convergence:} SAC converges significantly faster than AC (20 episodes vs. 60 episodes to reach 99\% accuracy)
    \item \textbf{Accuracy Rate:} Both algorithms achieve over 99\% AR on test set
    \item \textbf{Expert Data:} SAC more accurate on expert data (99.57\%) than AC (99.47\%)
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mean Square Error:} SAC achieves lower MSE (2.49) than AC (3.10) on test set
    \item \textbf{Accuracy Rate:} Both achieve 41.5\% AR on test set
    \item \textbf{Multiple Intubation Data:} SAC shows 6\% higher AR and 0.8 lower MSE than AC
    \item \textbf{Stability:} SAC more stable in final training episodes
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Key Findings}
\begin{itemize}
    \item SAC converges much faster than traditional AC algorithm
    \item SAC achieves higher data utilization efficiency

\section{Challenges in Reinforcement Learning for Personalized Medicine}

Despite significant progress, reinforcement learning (RL) in healthcare and personalized medicine faces several persistent challenges:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Data Quality and Availability:} Clinical datasets are often limited, noisy, incomplete, or imbalanced, which hinders robust RL model training and generalization \cite{yangDeepReinforcementLearning2024, killianEmpiricalStudyRepresentation2020, deshmukhReinforcementLearningHealthcare2024, luoReinforcementLearningDynamic2024, barataReinforcementLearningModel2023, hassouniClusteringbasedReinforcementLearning2020, mulaniDeepReinforcementLearning2020}.
    \item \textbf{Generalizability and Bias:} Models trained on single-center or homogeneous datasets may not generalize to diverse populations, raising concerns about fairness and bias \cite{ohEffectiveDatadrivenPrecision2022, banumathiReinforcementLearningPersonalized2025, banumathiReinforcementLearningPersonalized2025a, zadehOptimizingWarfarinDosing2023, zhengPersonalizedMultimorbidityManagement2021}.
    \item \textbf{Interpretability and Transparency:} Deep RL models are often ``black boxes,'' making it difficult for clinicians to understand, trust, and validate recommendations \cite{maDeepAttentionQnetwork2023, deshmukhReinforcementLearningHealthcare2024, barataReinforcementLearningModel2023, wuValuebasedDeepReinforcement2023, frommeyerReinforcementLearningIts2025, banumathiReinforcementLearningPersonalized2025}.
    \item \textbf{Reward Function Design:} Defining clinically meaningful, multi-objective reward functions that align with patient outcomes, safety, and quality of life is complex \cite{luoReinforcementLearningDynamic2024, frommeyerReinforcementLearningIts2025, petersenDeepReinforcementLearning2019, yangPrescDRLDeepReinforcement2024, zadehOptimizingWarfarinDosing2023}.
    \item \textbf{Safety and Ethics:} RL exploration can pose risks to patient safety; ethical constraints and regulatory requirements limit the scope of experimentation \cite{zhaoSafeenhancedFullyClosedloop2025, deshmukhReinforcementLearningHealthcare2024, mulaniDeepReinforcementLearning2020, gorrepatiReinforcementLearningFramework2025, yuSupervisedactorcriticReinforcementLearning2020}.
    \item \textbf{Computational Complexity and Scalability:} Training RL models on high-dimensional, multimodal clinical data requires significant computational resources and expertise \cite{DOI105425427552721, hassouniClusteringbasedReinforcementLearning2020, gorrepatiReinforcementLearningFramework2025, yangDeepReinforcementLearning2024}.
    \item \textbf{Offline Policy Evaluation and Validation:} Reliable evaluation of RL policies using retrospective data is challenging due to confounding, delayed effects, and lack of ground truth \cite{luoReinforcementLearningDynamic2024, killianEmpiricalStudyRepresentation2020, zhengPersonalizedMultimorbidityManagement2021, zadehOptimizingWarfarinDosing2023}.
    \item \textbf{Integration with Clinical Workflows:} Embedding RL systems into real-world clinical practice requires overcoming technical, regulatory, and workflow barriers \cite{barataReinforcementLearningModel2023, ohEffectiveDatadrivenPrecision2022, gorrepatiReinforcementLearningFramework2025, banumathiReinforcementLearningPersonalized2025}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Directions}

To address these challenges, future research should focus on:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Model Interpretability and Explainability:} Develop explainable RL models using XAI techniques (e.g., SHAP, LIME, attention mechanisms) to build clinician trust and facilitate adoption \cite{banumathiReinforcementLearningPersonalized2025, maDeepAttentionQnetwork2023, gorrepatiReinforcementLearningFramework2025, frommeyerReinforcementLearningIts2025}.
    \item \textbf{Federated and Privacy-Preserving Learning:} Employ federated RL and privacy-preserving data sharing to enable multi-center collaboration while protecting patient privacy \cite{banumathiReinforcementLearningPersonalized2025, abdellatifReinforcementLearningIntelligent2021, banumathiReinforcementLearningPersonalized2025a}.
    \item \textbf{Hybrid and Safe RL Algorithms:} Integrate supervised learning, expert knowledge, and safety constraints into RL frameworks to ensure reliable and clinically aligned decision-making \cite{wuValuebasedDeepReinforcement2023, yuSupervisedactorcriticReinforcementLearning2020, zhaoSafeenhancedFullyClosedloop2025, gorrepatiReinforcementLearningFramework2025}.
    \item \textbf{Advanced RL Methods:} Explore meta-RL, hierarchical RL, multi-agent RL, and digital twin simulations for more robust, adaptive, and personalized treatment strategies \cite{banumathiReinforcementLearningPersonalized2025, decarloReinforcementLearningPKPD2024, gorrepatiReinforcementLearningFramework2025, petersenDeepReinforcementLearning2019}.
    \item \textbf{Clinical Validation and Real-World Trials:} Move beyond retrospective studies to prospective, real-time clinical trials and integration with electronic medical record (EMR) systems \cite{zhengPersonalizedMultimorbidityManagement2021, gorrepatiReinforcementLearningFramework2025, barataReinforcementLearningModel2023, ohEffectiveDatadrivenPrecision2022}.
    \item \textbf{Reward Function Refinement:} Design multi-objective, patient-centered reward functions that balance efficacy, safety, cost, and quality of life \cite{luoReinforcementLearningDynamic2024, petersenDeepReinforcementLearning2019, yangPrescDRLDeepReinforcement2024, zadehOptimizingWarfarinDosing2023}.
    \item \textbf{Interdisciplinary Collaboration:} Foster collaboration among AI researchers, clinicians, ethicists, and policymakers to ensure responsible, effective, and ethical RL system design \cite{banumathiReinforcementLearningPersonalized2025, banumathiReinforcementLearningPersonalized2025a, frommeyerReinforcementLearningIts2025}.
    \item \textbf{Standardized Benchmarks and Taxonomies:} Develop standardized datasets, benchmarks, and taxonomies to improve reproducibility, comparability, and clinical relevance of RL research in healthcare \cite{frommeyerReinforcementLearningIts2025, abdellatifReinforcementLearningIntelligent2021}.
\end{itemize}

\section{A Primer on Reinforcement Learning in Medicine}
\textbf{Article Reference:} \cite{PrimerReinforcementLearning}

\subsection*{Overview}
This primer introduces reinforcement learning (RL) to clinicians, summarizing core concepts, clinical applications, implementation constraints, evaluation challenges, and promising research directions for integrating RL into medical practice.

\subsection*{Core Concepts}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Agent / Environment / Reward:} An RL agent interacts with an environment (patient state / clinical setting), selects actions and receives scalar rewards, learning a policy that maximizes cumulative reward.
    \item \textbf{MDP Framework:} Problems are commonly framed as Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) with states, actions, transition dynamics and rewards; limitations of the Markov assumption in medicine are noted.
    \item \textbf{Model-based vs Model-free:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Model-based RL learns or uses an environment model to plan.
        \item Model-free RL learns value or policy functions directly (value-based, policy-based, actor-critic hybrids).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Online vs Offline RL:} Safety and ethics usually preclude online exploration with real patients; offline (batch) RL on retrospective data is therefore the predominant clinical paradigm.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Clinical Applications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Critical care:} Treatment optimisation for sepsis (fluids/vasopressors), ventilator weaning, sedation, anticoagulation, and other ICU decisions where sequential control and dense monitoring exist.
    \item \textbf{Personalized dosing and therapies:} Insulin and artificial pancreas control, adaptive chemotherapy or targeted dosing using PK/PD simulators or digital twins.
    \item \textbf{Longitudinal care and chronic diseases:} Adaptive regimens for diabetes, hypertension, multimorbidity management and behavior-change interventions.
    \item \textbf{Robotics and automation:} Robotic-assisted surgery (RAS) and procedural automation where RL can learn fine-grained control policies from demonstrations and simulation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Major Challenges}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{State-space formulation:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item High-dimensional, noisy, irregular EHR data; missingness and preprocessing decisions strongly affect outcomes.
        \item Distribution shifts and demographic biases reduce generalizability.
        \item Partial observability and violation of Markov assumptions in clinical trajectories.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reward design:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Clinical objectives are multi-faceted (survival, quality of life, safety, cost) and often subjective.
        \item Sparse and delayed rewards (long-term outcomes) complicate credit assignment.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Action-space and safety:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Continuous vs discrete action choices, safety constraints (drug interactions, guideline adherence) and the need for constrained policies.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Evaluation and deployment:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Reliance on simulators and offline policy evaluation (OPE) with inherent biases.
        \item Scarcity of prospective clinical trials; regulatory, ethical and workflow integration barriers.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Future Directions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hybrid and safe RL:} Combine expert knowledge, supervised objectives and constrained RL to ensure safety and clinical alignment.
    \item \textbf{Federated and privacy-preserving methods:} Federated RL / FL-IRL to learn policies across institutions while maintaining patient privacy.
    \item \textbf{Integration with LLMs and multimodal models:} Use LLMs and multimodal encoders for richer state representations and clinician-facing explanations.
    \item \textbf{Digital twins and simulation:} High‑fidelity simulators and patient digital twins to pre-train and stress-test policies before clinical evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Real-time adaptive systems:} Transition from static prediction to actionable, bedside RL-based decision support with explainability and uncertainty quantification.
    \item \textbf{Robotics in surgery:} RL combined with advanced vision models to support automation and assistive behaviors in RAS.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Takeaway}
RL offers a principled framework for sequential, personalized decision-making in medicine but requires rigorous handling of data, safety constraints, reward design and evaluation. Progress depends on hybrid-safe algorithms, federated collaborations, realistic simulators, and prospective clinical validation to translate RL from promising research to safe clinical tools.

        \item \emph{Patient Model (Environment/Dynamics):} Learns a dynamics function (hidden-state generator) and a prediction function that outputs future clinical status (e.g., blood glucose, Within-Target-Range (WTR) probability).
        \item \emph{Policy Model (Agent):} Produces insulin dosing recommendations and performs multi-step planning by interacting with the learned patient model.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training regime:} Patient model trained with supervised objectives on observed transitions; policy optimized via model-based RL using the patient model for rollouts and with imitation/supervised loss components to reflect clinician behavior for safety.
    \item \textbf{Reward signal:} Estimated from the patient model; rewards encourage future states within target glycemic range (WTR defined as $3.9$--$10.0\ \mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$).
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{RL Component Definitions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{State:} Hidden patient state derived from temporal features (biochemistry, meds, insulin usage, admission context).
    \item \textbf{Action:} Recommended sequential insulin dose/titration (units).
    \item \textbf{Environment:} Learned patient model simulating glucose dynamics and reward estimates.
    \item \textbf{Agent:} Policy model optimized by RL + supervised imitation for safe dosing.
    \item \textbf{Reward:} Model-estimated indication of glucose being within target range (WTR); cumulative reward correlates with higher WTR ratio.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Results and Critique}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Performance:} Development MAE in insulin titration: $1.10\pm0.03$ U; clinical validation MAE: $1.18\pm0.09$ U (better than junior/intermediate physicians).
    \item \textbf{Clinical trial (proof-of-concept):} 16 T2D inpatients — mean daily capillary glucose decreased from $11.1\pm3.6$ to $8.6\pm2.4\ \mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ (P\!<\!0.01). No severe hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia with ketosis reported.
    \item \textbf{Critique:} Strong preliminary results; limitations include population homogeneity and inpatient-only context that may limit generalizability.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Challenges}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Generalizability:} Cohort mainly Han Chinese (= 92\%), requiring external validation across ethnicities and clinical settings.
    \item \textbf{Outpatient applicability:} Inpatient diet/activity are controlled; outpatient variability (diet, exercise) must be modelled for ambulatory use.
    \item \textbf{Safety:} Ensuring robust safety constraints and clinician-aligned dosing in diverse, noisy real-world data.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Future Work}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Broader multi-center prospective trials and external validation across ethnicities and care settings.
    \item Integration of patient-reported diet/exercise and wearable data for outpatient adaptation.
    \item Improved uncertainty estimation, safety layers, and clinician-in-the-loop deployment for real-world use.
\end{itemize}

\section{Personalized HeartSteps: A Reinforcement Learning Algorithm for Optimizing Physical Activity}
\textbf{Article Reference:} \cite{liaoPersonalizedHeartStepsReinforcement2020}
\subsection*{Overview}
Personalized HeartSteps is an online RL algorithm designed to optimize delivery of Just-In-Time Adaptive Interventions (JITAIs) to increase short-term physical activity. The system was developed and validated using two longitudinal studies (HeartSteps V1 pilot and HeartSteps V2 trial) and emphasizes rapid personalized learning while controlling for intervention burden.

\subsection*{Data and Preprocessing}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Datasets:} HeartSteps V1 (42-day pilot) used to build priors and a generative simulator; HeartSteps V2 (90-day trial) used for deployment and evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Reward transformation:} 30-minute post-decision step-count is log-transformed to reduce heavy tails and variance.
    \item \textbf{Dosage variable:} $X_t$ tracks recent intervention burden via exponential discounting; incremented when a walking/anti-sedentary suggestion was sent since the last decision time. Used as a proxy for habituation/adverse long-term effects.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Reinforcement Learning Approach}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Algorithm:} Bayesian Thompson Sampling (TS) with informative priors from V1 to accelerate per-user learning and balance exploration–exploitation.
    \item \textbf{Update cadence:} Policy parameters updated nightly using accumulated interaction data.
    \item \textbf{Stochasticization and clipping:} Randomization probability $\pi_t$ is clipped to $[\epsilon_1,1-\epsilon_0]$ to ensure sufficient data in both action arms for off-policy analysis.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{MDP Components}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Action $A_t$:} Binary — send activity suggestion ($1$) or not ($0$); decisions made five times per day.
    \item \textbf{State $S_t=\{I_t,Z_t,X_t\}$:}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item $I_t$ (availability): whether an intervention is feasible (e.g., not driving).
            \item $Z_t$ (context): features such as location, prior 30-min step count, yesterday's steps, temperature, recent activity history.
            \item $X_t$ (dosage): treatment-burden proxy.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reward $R_t$:} $\log(\text{steps}_{t+30\text{min}}+1)$ (log-transformed 30-min step count).
    \item \textbf{Delayed-effect proxy $\eta_d(x)$:} Estimated cost of habituation derived from $X_t$; used to compare immediate benefit against long-term burden.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Implementation Details}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Action-centering:} Reward model centers actions to protect against baseline nonstationarity and model misspecification.
    \item \textbf{Informative prior and simulator:} V1-derived priors and a V1-based simulator enable faster cold-start learning in V2.
    \item \textbf{Per-user personalization:} Fully personalized policies learned per participant, with options for pooling similarity-based priors in future work.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Critique and Challenges}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Burden estimation:} Dosage is a low-variance proxy but may under-capture complex engagement dynamics; richer latent-burden models are desirable.
    \item \textbf{Model simplicity:} Linear, low-dimensional reward models were chosen for stability with limited pilot data; richer models could improve performance but need careful regularization.
    \item \textbf{Non-stationarity:} Change-point detection and online tuning of discount parameters would improve adaptation to temporal shifts in behavior.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Future Work}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Develop latent-variable or hierarchical models for burden/engagement to better capture delayed effects.
    \item Explore partial pooling or clustering to share information across similar users and speed learning.
    \item Implement online tuning and change-point detection to handle non-stationarity more responsively.
    \item Extend evaluation with rigorous off-policy analyses and longer-term engagement studies.
\end{itemize}

\section{MedDreamer: Model-Based Reinforcement Learning with Latent Imagination on Complex EHRs for Clinical Decision Support}
\textbf{Article Reference:} \cite{PDFMedDreamerModelBased}
\subsection*{Overview}
MedDreamer is a two-phase model-based Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework that learns a compact latent world model from irregular, sparse EHRs and then trains a policy by performing ``latent imagination'' (simulated rollouts) inside that model. The method targets robust, generalizable treatment policies for tasks such as sepsis therapy and mechanical ventilation management.

\subsection*{Datasets and Preprocessing}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Datasets:} Evaluated on MIMIC-IV (sepsis treatment) and eICU (mechanical ventilation management).
    \item \textbf{Adaptive Feature Integration (AFI):} Rather than discretizing/imputing, AFI encodes each clinical feature together with the elapsed time since its last observation, enabling the model to process irregularly sampled, sparse EHR streams directly.
    \item \textbf{Representation:} Raw features + elapsed-time channels are fed into the world model to produce temporally-aware latent states.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{MedDreamer RL Architecture}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{World Model (Environment):} A Recurrent State-Space Model (RSSM) augmented with AFI that maps observation sequences to a dense latent state capturing patient dynamics.
    \item \textbf{Agent / Policy:} Neural policy trained in latent space via imagined rollouts produced by the world model.
    \item \textbf{State:} Latent state vector from the RSSM summarizing recent history and implicitly modelling missingness/timing.
    \item \textbf{Action:} Task-specific treatment recommendations (e.g., discrete joint IV/vasopressor bins or ventilation settings).
    \item \textbf{Reward:} Learned reward estimator (from outcomes) used during imagination to assign immediate and long-term value to imagined transitions.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Two-Phase Latent Imagination Policy Training}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Phase 1 — Imagine through Time ($\tau$):} Ground the policy on short-horizon predictions by training on real trajectories augmented with short imagined rollouts (\(\tau=10\) steps). This stabilizes learning and aligns the policy with observed clinical dynamics.
    \item \textbf{Phase 2 — Imagine through Horizon ($H$):} Refine the policy with longer counterfactual simulations (\(H=15\) steps) in the learned model to explore downstream consequences and improve robustness to unseen scenarios.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Key Findings}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Performance:} Reported estimated relative mortality reduction of \(\approx20.9\%\) for sepsis and \(\approx16\%\) for mechanical ventilation tasks versus baselines (evaluated via model-estimated returns / OPE).
    \item \textbf{AFI impact:} Explicit elapsed-time modelling (AFI) improved generalization to irregular EHRs compared to discretize-and-impute baselines.
    \item \textbf{Stability:} The two-phase imagination curriculum (short then long rollouts) yielded more clinically-aligned and stable policies.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Critique and Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Policies are learned from observational data and evaluated using a learned reward model and offline policy evaluation (OPE), which may not perfectly reflect true clinical effect.
    \item External validity beyond MIMIC-IV/eICU and across hospitals, populations, and deployment conditions remains to be established.
    \item Practical deployment requires clinician-facing explainability, rigorous safety constraints, and prospective validation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Future Work}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Validate generalization across multi-center cohorts and prospective clinical studies.
    \item Improve reward realism (reduce dependence on learned reward models) and develop rigorous safety layers for clinical deployment.
    \item Study clinician trust and interpretability mechanisms for latent-space policies and imagined trajectories.
\end{itemize}
